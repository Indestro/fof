# Back the entry cheque, not the follow-on

**The structure (2026-08-17).** A committed seed fund that deploys through a bench of proven angels
who have no vehicle of their own. They pick and get the allocation; we supply the capital, the rails
and the LP base. Not a fund-of-funds, and not co-investment either.

## Why this shape and not the other two

**Not a fund-of-funds.** We cannot buy the funds that measure well — Chapter One is not raising, SV
Angel and Foundation and Amplify are closed. We cannot attribute the ones we can buy — Humba,
Chemistry, Category and Theory have no scoreable history, and Silent Ventures' 2.70x turned out to be
one man's personal angel record wearing a fund's name. And where a direct comparison exists, the
people beat the vehicle that pools them: **Village Global's fund measures 1.89x while Belsky is
2.75x, Hartz 3.10x, Patel 3.10x.** A fund's record is its partners' records with the attribution
stripped out, sold back to us with a fee on top.

**Not SPVs into their follow-on rounds.** This was the first version of the plan and it was wrong.
Mei's book throws off ~12 rounds of $5M+ a year — Etched at $500M, Exa at $250M, MatX at $500M. But
buying those captures **his brand, not his edge**. His edge was entering at seed in 2024 at a price
he helped set. A cheque into the Series C at a multi-billion valuation is an ordinary late-stage
investment any wealth platform can source. We would be paying to follow someone into a price they
had already made.

**The edge lives at the entry cheque.** So we fund the entry cheque.

## How it works

- A committed pool. Six to ten angels on the bench, each with an annual allocation to deploy at
  their own discretion, at seed, into their own picks.
- They keep meaningful carry on their own deals. No management fee to them — they are not managing.
- We retain final approval. This is not a courtesy: it is the adverse-selection defence, it is what
  keeps investment discretion with the manager for regulatory purposes, and it is where our own
  signal enters — we only fund what independently clears it.

**Capacity, which is the arithmetic that makes it work.** An angel gets $100-250k of allocation in a
hot seed; they cannot hand us $1M. So we do not ask them to. Give one angel $2-4M a year to write
their own cheques and we are behind every one at entry price. Six angels is **$15-25M a year**,
which deploys a $40M fund over roughly three years — a normal seed pace.

At ~$200k average cheque that is a wide book, 80-120 positions. That is correct for seed: the
returns are a power law, breadth is how you catch the tail, and **the alpha is in the selection, not
the concentration.**

## The economics with the angel

Market anchor is the scout programmes — Sequoia and a16z historically gave scouts about half the
carry on their own deals. These are not junior scouts, so the number should be better: **50-60% of
carry on their deals, no management fee.**

**Why an angel takes it** rather than writing their own cheque and keeping everything: their own
money is the binding constraint. We are offering to multiply their cheque by ten or more. Bigger
cheques mean more ownership, more influence with the founder, and better allocation next time. No
fundraising work. And a path to their own fund with us as anchor in two years.

The pitch in one line: *you are picking well with $25k — we will make it $250k, you keep half the
upside on the difference, and we build you a fund when you want one.*

## The risk that decides it

**Can the angel convert a $25k invitation into a $250k allocation?** A founder lets an angel in for
$25k for their name and their help. $250k is a real allocation decision that competes with the lead.
If the answer is no, the whole structure caps out at a size that cannot support a fund.

This is answerable by asking, and it should be the first question in every conversation — before
track record, before terms. Three other risks, all manageable: adverse selection (fixed by our
approval right plus our own signal), behaviour change when spending someone else's money at half
the carry, and the regulatory question of where discretion sits (fixed by us holding final approval).

## What this changes about the Gedik pitch

The fund-of-funds wrapper survives, because a listed group's investment committee can approve a
known category with published benchmarks. But the direct sleeve stops being a co-investment
afterthought and becomes the engine, dialled up as the record accumulates.

**And it inverts a modelling assumption we should now test.** The simulator currently penalises a
larger direct sleeve — 35% to 60% takes returns from 2.57x to 2.34x — because informed co-investment
was calibrated at 2.20x against a measured 1.27x for random. If these angels genuinely pick at 3x
the base rate and we only fund what clears our own signal, the direct sleeve should be the better
half, not the worse one. The model currently argues against the thesis on a number I invented.
