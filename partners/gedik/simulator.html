We find founders before the top venture firms do — and we can reach them

So the fund does not buy positions in other people's funds. It backs the individuals who pick best, at the moment they write the cheque. We supply the capital and the feed; they supply the judgement and the invitation.

Olympos × Gedik · $40M first close, Gedik anchoring $20M · August 2026 · market-structure analysis published at emrahyalaz.com/#vc-conviction

The whole thing, in six sentences.

Twenty-nine venture firms invested in 82% of all new unicorns, by Series A, since January 2022. When one arrives the price jumps — and a company that draws one within two years of its seed is five times more likely to go on and raise $100M.

We can see them coming. We flag founders while they are still in stealth, and for the ones a top firm later funds, our flag came a median of nine months first.

A small number of individuals are unusually good at getting into those companies at seed. We can now measure exactly who, from records rather than reputation.

Almost none of them has a fund. So the fund gives them capital to write bigger cheques, they keep most of the upside, and we sit behind every one of their picks at the entry price.

What on this page is measured, and what is assumed. Measured — the seven verified cases where our flag preceded a professional investor; the 137 conversions out of 5,323 flagged founders; the 5.88% base rate across 26,636 companies and every individual's rate against it; what a tier-one arrival is worth, from 15,090 companies seeded 2019-21; the sector head starts. Assumed — that these individuals can convert a $25k invitation into a $250k allocation; that they would accept the arrangement; that a hit rate measured on small personal cheques holds at ten times the size. The first is what decides whether this is a business, and it is answered by asking, not by modelling.

In order: what we saw first, whether it holds at scale, what the signal is worth, who picks best, where being early pays, how the fund would back them, what it returns, and the structure.

2The lead holds across 5,323 founders, not just seven

Seven cases could be luck. So we took every founder the system has ever flagged — 5,329 signals, 5,323 distinct people, 5,290 matched to a profile — found their company, and asked whether one of the 29 firms later invested. It happened 137 times, 2.6% of those flagged, and for those the median gap was 8.6 months.

This is the keystone. 4We rank people by what they picked, not funds by reputation

The usual way to rank early investors is to count how often they got in before a top firm. That ranks whoever writes the most cheques. Put a denominator on it — out of everything you seeded, what share drew top-firm money within two years — and across 26,636 companies the answer is 5.88%. That is the bar.

Aerospace is where we would extend. The only sector with a wide head start and rising demand from the top firms — deal count 28 to 41 in a year, the biggest proportional rise on the board, median cheque $20M to $50M, and 7 new US unicorns in 2026. Two sectors we would not build for at all: in robotics and crypto the top firms are at seed before the feeder layer, so there is nothing to get ahead of. And a warning about our own ground — in infrastructure the top firms cut their time from seed to Series A from 481 days to 283 in one year. Our head start is real, and it is closing fastest in the lane we source hardest.

6The fund backs the entry cheque, not the follow-on

The obvious move is to invest alongside these people in the big rounds their companies later raise — Etched at $500M, Exa at $250M. That is the wrong trade. It buys their brand, not their edge: the edge was entering at seed at a price they helped set, and a cheque into the Series C is an ordinary late-stage investment anyone can source.

So the fund backs the entry cheque. Each person on the bench receives an annual allocation to deploy at their own discretion, at seed, into their own picks. They keep most of the carry on their own deals and pay no management fee, because they are not managing anything. The fund keeps final approval — which is the defence against being handed only the deals they could not fill themselves, and the point at which our own signal enters.

The arithmetic. One of these people gets $100–250k of allocation in a hot seed; they cannot hand the fund $1M. So we do not ask. Give one person $2–4M a year to write their own cheques and the fund sits behind every one at entry price. Six people is $15–25M a year — the right pace for a $40M fund, across 80 to 120 positions, which is correct when returns are a power law and the edge is in selection rather than concentration.

The question that decides everything, and it is not in the model. Can a $25k invitation become a $250k allocation? A founder lets an angel in for $25k for their name and their help; $250k is a real allocation decision competing with the lead. If the answer is no, the structure caps below fund size and nothing else on this page matters. It is answered by asking, and it is the first question in every conversation.

The near-term work is verification, not conviction. Mei Zuo's record is self-reported and has to be checked against filings before it counts. The capacity question — whether a $25k invitation becomes a $250k allocation — has to be asked of every name before any of them is a line in a fund. That is the right order: measure, then meet, then commit.

Model notes and what would change these numbers

The company model. Every position resolves one of two ways: a new top-29 firm arrives within two years of the seed, or it does not. The probability is the base 7.99% multiplied by the bench's measured hit rate. The outcome distribution in each branch is a power law fitted so the branch means reproduce the section-3 conditionals — the tier-one branch produces $100M+ outcomes about a quarter of the time, the other branch mostly does not raise again.

Persistence. The bench is the top of 122 measured individuals, so its rates are in-sample maxima. We tested how much survives: rates fitted on 2019-21 seed entries and scored on 2022-23, across 272 investors. Monotone — the top fit quartile went on to 1.95x the base, the second 1.59x, the third 1.16x, the bottom 0.65x, a threefold spread out of sample. At bench size the coefficient is 0.87, so the model discounts to 85%. The aggregate persists; individuals do not — inside that same top group SV Angel went 3.31x to 5.28x while Naval Ravikant went 2.35x to zero on 29 bets. That dispersion is the argument for a bench rather than one person.

Fees. 20% carry and 2% a year on committed capital over ten years, taken off the gross. There is no second fee layer, because the fund invests directly rather than buying positions in other funds — worth roughly 25% of terminal value against a fund-of-funds structure.

Vintage risk is regime-switching, not lognormal. A thin-tailed draw cannot produce a 2000 or a 2021, yet both happened inside 25 years. 18% of vintages get a bust regime centred near a 55% haircut. This is the dominant driver of the chance of losing money, and no amount of selection diversifies it away.

What is assumed rather than measured. That these individuals can secure a materially larger allocation than they take today; that they would accept the arrangement; and that a hit rate measured on small personal cheques holds at ten times the cheque size. None of the three is established by anything above, and the first is the one to test first.
